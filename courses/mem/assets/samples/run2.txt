  1. THE INSTRUMENT, and the two kinds of memory measurement
  ------------------------------------------------------
     The core clock, sampled from /proc/cpuinfo six times over
     this section, ranged 1397 to 2844 MHz -- a 104% swing.
     An L1 hit costs a fixed number of CORE CYCLES, so its cost
     in NANOSECONDS moves with that clock, and the absolute
     spread below is mostly the clock rather than the memory.
     (A SAMPLE, not a live reading -- the previous course lost a
     factor of three by treating it as one.)

     The noise floor is the spread of the ESTIMATOR: the whole
     min-of-3 procedure, repeated 14 times, on the same 64-line
     chase.  This is the quantity every tolerance below is
     applied to, so this is the quantity worth measuring.

       14 min-of-3 values, 1.440 to 1.877 ns, mean 1.725
       spread about the mean:  25.3%   <-- the noise floor

     For contrast, the 42 INDIVIDUAL runs behind those minima
     run from 1.440 to 2.000 ns, a spread of 31.7% -- 1.3x
     worse.  Quoting that larger number is the mistake this
     section exists to correct, and it is a mistake the previous
     course made: it reported 15% for a quantity that is really
     this one, and then had to loosen tolerances by hand.

     Every load-bearing number in this program is a RATIO, so
     what constrains them is the spread of a RATIO.  Two arms
     differing by a known large factor, 12 rounds, 3 samples
     per round, the two arms measured back to back:

       estimator                           low       high   spread
       arm A alone (L1, ns)              1.627      1.779     8.8%
       arm B alone (L2, ns)             18.759     21.655    13.9%
       min(B)/min(A)                    10.544     13.031    20.6%
       min of PAIRED ratios              9.987     12.174    19.3%

     A REFUTED HYPOTHESIS, kept because it is the reason the
     estimator is the one below.  Ratios are supposed to cancel
     common-mode error, so a ratio ought to be steadier than its
     operands.  min(B)/min(A) is not: 20.6% here, against the L1
     arm's own 8.8%, and the gap widens when the clock is moving
     faster -- an earlier run of this same section had a core clock
     swinging 149% and gave 24.0% for the ratio against 17.0% for
     the arm.  The reason is structural, not accidental: one
     minimum is taken at one moment and the other at a different
     one, so the drift between them multiplies into the quotient
     instead of cancelling.

     The PAIRED estimator removes the reason rather than the
     symptom: divide inside the iteration, before the clock has
     moved on, and only then take a minimum.  19.3%, which is
     1.1x steadier than the ratio of minima.

     And HERE THAT WORD IS NOT GUARANTEED.  Across the runs
     this section has been through, the paired estimator was
     steadier about twenty times out of twenty-four, and worse
     the other four.  The ordering is a property of how hard the
     clock happened to be moving during a given run, not of the
     estimator: pairing is right by construction, because the
     division is the only place the two arms' clock drift can
     still cancel, but on a quiet run there is little drift left
     to cancel and the two estimators come out the same.  So a
     harness that asserted the ordering would fail on the quiet
     runs, and one that asserted the opposite would fail on the
     other twenty.  Assert the REASON, not the ordering.

     That is the number the tolerances below come from, and it is
     why every table in this program carries a ratio column and
     why no absolute latency is claimed anywhere.
     The tolerances use the WIDEST of the three spreads, 20.6%, 
     rather than one of them by name.  This run's three are
     13.9% for the noisier arm, 20.6% for the ratio of minima
     and 19.3% for the paired one; the steadiest of them here
     is the L1 arm.  Which one that is MOVES between runs
     -- the paired estimator was steadier in about twenty runs
     out of twenty-four and worse in the other four, and that
     is a property of how hard the clock was moving rather
     than of the estimator -- so a sentence naming one of them
     is false on the next run, and a check asserting the
     ordering fails on a quiet clock.  Take the widest, and
     assert the reason rather than the ordering.

     A pointer chase and a sequential walk look like two ways of
     doing the same thing.  They are not, and the difference is
     the whole subject of section 3:

       chase: the next address is READ FROM MEMORY, so it cannot
              be known until the previous load has returned
              -- this measures LATENCY

       sweep: the next address is base + i*64, computable long
              before the load issues
              -- this measures BANDWIDTH

     A benchmark that is one and not the other measures the wrong
     one, and nothing in the output says which it did.

  3. LINE GRANULARITY, AND THE PREFETCHER AS A DIFFERENCE
  ----------------------------------------------------
     Same footprint, same machine, two addresses:

     A 64 MiB working set, 1,048,576 lines, entirely outside
     every level of the cache:

     pointer chase, random order           132.627 ns     0.483 GB/s
     sequential sweep, +64 each time         3.464 ns    18.474 GB/s
     the prefetcher, by subtraction         38.28x

     The two move the same 64 bytes per line, touch the same
     64 MiB, and hit the same DRAM.  They differ in exactly one
     respect: whether the address of the next access can be
     computed before the current one has returned.  The 38.3x
     between them IS the prefetcher, and there is no way to see
     it directly without a PMU -- you can only subtract it.

     This is the single most useful number in the course and
     it is the reason a random walk over 64 MiB costs 133 ns a
     step while a sequential read of the same 64 MiB costs 3.46
     ns a line.  Same memory. Same machine. Different addresses.

     And what a line buys, at strides from one 4-byte element
     per 4096-byte page up to a full line per element:

       stride     elements     ns/element      useful GB/s      line GB/s
            4      4194304         0.4540             8.81           8.81
            8      2097152         0.5648             7.08          14.16
           16      1048576         0.7539             5.31          21.22
           32       524288         1.1784             3.39          27.16
           64       262144         1.8318             2.18          34.94
          128       131072         2.4377             1.64          26.25
          256        65536         2.5261             1.58          25.34
         1024        16384         4.9448             0.81          12.94
         4096         4096         9.1327             0.44           7.01

     'useful GB/s' counts the 4 bytes each element really
     wanted.  'line GB/s' counts the 64-byte lines the memory
     system had to move to supply them.  At a 4096-byte
     stride the system moved 256 KiB of lines to deliver
     16 KiB of wanted data -- 16 to 1 -- and every one of
     those lines was in a different 4 KiB page as well.

     The useful rate collapses across the range, 8.81 down to
     0.44 GB/s, so the sparsest row is 20 times slower than
     the densest even though both are reading the same
     16 MiB -- and it is NOT monotonic row by row.  At the
     dense end that is the LOOP rather than the memory:
     strides 4 and 8 both use every byte of every line they
     fetch, so their two rows differ only in the loop's own
     per-element overhead, and they swap order between runs.
     The line rate is not monotonic either, for a different
     reason -- it peaks in the middle -- which is the first
     sign that the two columns are different quantities and
     must not be compared with each other.

     Across the DENSE range the LINE rate rises 4.0x, from 8.81 GB/s
     at stride 4 to 34.94 at stride 64, because a dense walk
     fetches the same 16 MiB of lines whatever the stride and
     uses more of each one as the stride grows.  It is not
     monotone at every step either -- the peak is at stride 32
     or 64 depending on the run, which is the loop floor again --
     and the useful column falls, so the gap between the two is
     the 16 to 1 above.  They are different quantities and
     neither is a bandwidth.

  6. WRITES: what a store to a line you do not hold costs
  -------------------------------------------------
     Sequential sweep, addresses known in advance, 4 lines per
     iteration so the loop is not the bottleneck.  ns per LINE.
     'st64' writes 64 bytes of each line, 'st4' writes 4.

          KiB      read      st64       st4    NTfull    NTpart   st64/rd    st4/rd
           16     0.274     0.509     0.509    17.062     4.148     1.86x     1.86x
           64     0.890     0.949     0.969    17.140     3.737     1.07x     1.09x
          256     0.880     0.951     0.951    11.437     2.830     1.08x     1.08x
         1024     1.042     1.306     1.262    11.230     2.807     1.25x     1.21x
         4096     1.038     1.329     1.329    15.296     2.803     1.28x     1.28x
        16384     2.639     3.470     3.573    14.537     4.316     1.32x     1.35x
        32768     3.050     6.485     6.746    15.178     3.061     2.13x     2.21x
        65536     3.680     8.940     8.981    15.678     4.132     2.43x     2.44x

     A store to a line the CPU does not hold has to bring the
     line in first, or the bytes it is not writing would be
     lost.  That is write-allocate, and it predicts a store-only
     sweep must move TWICE the traffic of a read-only sweep.
     Below the 16 MiB last-level cache the ratio peaks at
     1.32x, over the sizes from 256 KiB up to the cache: both
     arms are running out of CACHE there, not out of bandwidth.
     Above it, 2.43x, and the step happens at the size of the
     last level of the hierarchy, which is where it has to.
     The two SMALLEST footprints are excluded from the band
     because their ratio is the LOOP's and not the memory's --
     at 16 KiB a read sweep finishes a line in under a fifth of
     a nanosecond, which is the loop and not a memory access,
     and no store can match that for reasons that have nothing
     to do with DRAM.  Including them made the band 1.80x on a
     run whose step was 2.38x, and a check about a step then
     failed on a step that was plainly there.

     st4 and st64 are the same to within the noise at every
     size.  Writing 4 bytes to a line costs what writing 64
     does, because the transaction is the LINE: you are charged
     for fetching all 64 bytes and writing all 64 bytes back
     whatever you used.

     The two NT columns are the interesting ones.  A
     non-temporal store does not allocate, so it should be
     EXPENSIVE at a small footprint -- every store is a trip to
     DRAM -- and cheap at a large one.  Measured: expensive at
     all of them, and flat.  16 KiB, entirely inside the L3,
     costs 17.062 ns per line.  65536 KiB, four times the L3, costs
     15.678.  The smaller footprint is not cheaper.  The flatness is
     the proof of non-allocation: nothing on that path consults
     the cache, so no footprint can help it -- and the folklore
     advice to use non-temporal stores for streaming writes does
     not hold on this machine.

  8. WHAT WAS RETRACTED
  ---------------------
     1. "The L1d aliasing stride is 4096 bytes, so 256 lines at
        4096 bytes will show a 5.28x TLB cost."  The 5.28x was real
        and the interpretation was wrong: the layout put those
        lines at offsets 64*(i mod 8), so 256 lines shared 8 SETS
        of an 8-way cache.  It was the section-4 conflict miss,
        measured a page at a time.

     2. "There is a TLB cliff at 32 pages."  Also wrong, same
        cause: one line per 4096-byte page is exactly the aliasing
        stride, so every page's line was in L1 set 0.  The cliff
        disappeared when the offset spread over all 64 sets, and
        the real cliff is at 64 pages, found by bisection in
        section 5.
     3. "Sequential reads sustain 64 GB/s."  The units were
        wrong AND the loop was the bottleneck: the first version
        measured 3.30 ns per line at EVERY footprint from 256 KiB
        to 64 MiB, and a rate that is the same for L2-resident and
        for DRAM data is the rate of a loop, not of a memory.
        With four lines per iteration: 1.739 ns per line over a
        4 MiB L2-resident region and 4.136 ns over 64 MiB of DRAM,
        i.e. 36.8 and 15.5 GB/s.  THE SWEEP SATURATES, and this is
        the honest limit of the measurement rather than a property
        worth teaching as one: at 3 ns for four 16-byte loads the
        loop is a real part of the cost, so this instrument cannot
        report the bandwidth of an L2-resident region.  The DRAM
        figure it does report is 15.5 GB/s, and the cache-resident
        figure comes from section 6's smallest row instead.
     4. "A non-temporal store is 16.4 ns per 16 bytes."  That was
        real, and it was a bug: the navigation load and the NT
        store shared a cache line, so the load allocated the very
        line the NT store was supposed to bypass.  The target moved
        2 MiB away and the number stayed high -- for a different
        reason, which is the flat line in section 6.
     5. "False sharing has no effect here."  The two threads
        were reading one counter, and then two barriers of count
        two with one waiter each DEADLOCKED the benchmark.  Fixed
        twice; the result is the factor of 89.5 in section 7.
     6. Three separate benchmark segfaults, all from writing past
        a mapping: an element at i*4096 + 64*(i mod 512) runs off
        the end of the last page, and a size loop that computed
        reps = MAXL/lines with lines > MAXL produced reps = 0 and
        an infinite loop that walked forward until it faulted.
     7. rdpmc does not exist as a thing you can just call.  The
        first version of section 0 executed it and killed the
        benchmark, taking every measurement with it.  It now runs
        in a forked child and reports the signal.
     8. "The 256 MiB row measures 8 ns."  Physically absurd,
        and the reason was an OFFSET: the layout added
        64*(i mod 64) bytes to line i, which is 128i for i < 64, so
        line 32 landed on line 64's address and line 64 on line
        128's.  A 256 MiB layout really touched a few megabytes.  The
        permutation still visited every INDEX, so every self-check
        passed and the table was nonsense.  The check that catches it
        is in group B: walk the cycle and COUNT the distinct
        addresses instead of trusting the index count.  The same
        bug then reappeared as 4096*(i mod 64), a stray * 64 in the
        same expression, and produced a TLB table whose cost was
        HIGH for 32 pages and LOW for 72 -- the exact inverse of the
        truth.  A non-monotonic table is not noise.  It is a signal
        that the layout is not what the caption says it is.

     9. A register named in an asm template is NOT a
        scratch register.  "movl (...), eax" was, and it
        is whatever the register allocator gave to an operand, and
        here it gave %rax to the loop counter, so the generated code
        was
            mov -0x18(%rbp),%rax   <- the counter, 4194304
            mov (%rdx),%eax        <- CLOBBERS the counter
            dec %rax
        and the walk ran until some 4-byte word happened to be zero.
        Two earlier "fixes" made it worse: an earlyclobber marker
        on p moved the collision instead of removing it, and a
        literal-immediate stride removed one hazard and left this
        one.  The rule, learned in three attempts: every register an
        asm template NAMES must be a declared operand or a declared
        clobber, and there is no third case.
